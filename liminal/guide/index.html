Liminal 1.1.0 · user guide

The Liminal manual

Everything in Liminal, from installing it to the last button: what each control does, every mode it has, when to reach for it and when not to. Liminal is built to be used by hand: pick a style, pull the threshold down until it sounds right, and the ceiling keeps the peaks safe. The Tools button adds a second opinion when you want one.

How to read this guide. The pictures are the real plug-in. The numbers on them match the numbered lists underneath, so you can find any control at a glance. New to Liminal? Read Quick start, then come back to the rest when you need it. In the plug-in itself, hover over any control for a short explanation.

1. Install

Download Liminal 1.1.0 free from the Liminal page. Windows may show Windows protected your PC because Liminal isn't code-signed yet: click More info, then Run anyway. On a Mac, if macOS says it can't check the installer, open System Settings → Privacy & Security and click Open Anyway.

Windows

  1. Run the installer. It copies Liminal.vst3 to C:\Program Files\Common Files\VST3, the standard folder every VST3 host looks in. If you have a zip instead, copy Liminal.vst3 into that folder yourself.
  2. Open your DAW and let it rescan plug-ins (most do this on start-up; in some you need to press Rescan in the plug-in settings).
  3. Find Liminal under the maker MasterScope, in the Dynamics or Effects category.

On Windows, Liminal needs Windows 10 or 11, 64-bit, and a DAW that loads VST3 plug-ins: Ableton Live, FL Studio, Cubase, Reaper, Studio One, Bitwig and most others.

macOS

  1. Open Liminal-1.1.0-macOS.pkg. Liminal isn't notarised by Apple yet, so the first time macOS may say it can't check the installer: open System Settings → Privacy & Security, scroll down, click Open Anyway, then open the installer again.
  2. It installs Liminal.vst3 to /Library/Audio/Plug-Ins/VST3 and Liminal.component (the Audio Unit) to /Library/Audio/Plug-Ins/Components. Untick either under Customise if you only want one.
  3. Open your DAW. Logic and GarageBand check new Audio Units when they start; in other hosts, rescan plug-ins. Find Liminal under MasterScope.

Liminal runs natively on Apple silicon and Intel Macs with macOS 10.13 or later, in any VST3 or AU host: Logic Pro, Ableton Live, FL Studio, Cubase, Reaper, Studio One, Bitwig and others. Measuring "this computer's sound" is Windows-only; on a Mac, measure through the track's input.

Uninstall

Delete Liminal.vst3 from the VST3 folder (on a Mac, also Liminal.component from /Library/Audio/Plug-Ins/Components). Your own presets are kept in Documents\MasterScope\Liminal\Presets (on a Mac, ~/Documents/MasterScope/Liminal/Presets), so reinstalling brings them back. Delete that folder too if you want them gone.

2. Quick start

Five minutes from a finished mix to a master you can trust.

  1. Put Liminal last on your master bus, after any EQ, compression or saturation. Nothing that changes the level should come after it, or the peaks it controlled can come back.
  2. Pick a style. Lookahead (the default) suits most music. Or start from a preset such as Streaming: punchy (PLR 10).
  3. Play the loudest part of the song and pull the Threshold down until it's as loud as you want and still sounds right. Auto makeup turns the output up as you go, and the Ceiling (−1.0 dBTP) keeps the peaks safe.
  4. Watch the Loudness card. It shows the integrated loudness, the true peak and the PLR (how dense the master is). For streaming, a PLR of 9 to 11 is punchy and competitive.
  5. Listen honestly. Turn on Level match to hear your unlimited mix at the same loudness, and Hear what's removed to hear only what the limiter takes away. If the drums lose their snap, back the threshold off or try Punch Guard.
  6. Want a second opinion? Open Tools, choose a target and press Learn, play the song, then press Stop: Liminal suggests the threshold that lands on it. See Learn and targets.
  7. Bounce your master as usual. Optional: press Report (beside Learn) and choose Open in MasterScope to check it against every platform in your browser.

3. A tour of the window

Liminal's window has six areas. Everything you need while mastering is on the main screen; settings you set once and leave are folded away under Advanced, and the helpers live under Tools.

  1. Top bar. Presets, save and delete, undo and redo, A/B, the Tools panel, theme, full screen and About. More.
  2. Display. What the limiter is doing, over time. Tabs switch between Limiter, Loudness and History, plus whichever tool you opened. The two listening switches sit above it on the right. More.
  3. Style controls. The Style menu and the knobs for that style, with Adaptive release and Bass first. More.
  4. Meters. Threshold, gain reduction (GR) and Ceiling, then Auto makeup and Output. More.
  5. Loudness card. Integrated loudness, short-term, true peak and PLR. More opens every reading. More.
  6. Bottom bar. Advanced, with a summary of its settings, the stereo width and correlation meters, latency, Snapshot and Reset meters. More.

4. The top bar

  1. Previous preset. Steps back through the preset list.
  2. Preset menu. Click for the full list: the 14 factory presets, your own presets, and Import preset at the bottom. Hover over it to read what the current preset is for. See Presets and A/B.
  3. Next preset. Steps forward.
  4. Save. Saves the current settings as a preset of your own. Your presets live in Documents\MasterScope\Liminal\Presets (on a Mac, ~/Documents/MasterScope/Liminal/Presets), so they survive reinstalling and are easy to back up or share.
  5. Delete. Deletes the selected preset of yours. Factory presets can't be deleted.
  6. Undo. Takes back your last change, including Learn, Apply in Optimise, loading a preset and importing one. Each is one step.
  7. Redo. Puts back a change you undid.
  1. A / B. Two complete sets of settings. Click the other letter to switch; changes you make stay in the slot you were on. Use it to compare two styles, two thresholds or two presets on the same passage. Tip: turn on Level match in both, or compare at the same Learn target, so the louder one doesn't win by being louder.
  2. Tools. Opens the Tools panel: Learn at the top, then Optimise, Mix Doctor, Spectrum, Spectrogram, Measure a song, Reference track, Codec check and Session log. Nothing in Tools changes your sound until you say so. See Learn and targets.
  3. Theme. Dark, Light, or one of three hardware looks: Console, Rack and Vintage. The screens stay the same in every theme, so the readings are always easy to read. See Themes and window.
  4. Full screen. Fits the window to your screen; click again to go back.
  5. About (ⓘ). The version, links to this guide and What's new, the licences, and how to support Liminal.

5. The display

The big display shows what the limiter is doing to the last few seconds of music. It's the quickest way to see whether you're catching a few peaks or squashing everything.

  1. View tabs. Limiter, Loudness and History are always there. When you open a tool (Spectrum, Mix Doctor and so on) it appears as a fourth tab, so you can flick between it and the limiter.
  2. Hear what's removed. Plays only what the limiter is taking away. See Listening honestly.
  3. Level match. Plays your unlimited mix at the same loudness as the output. See Listening honestly.
  4. The Limiter view. The last 10 seconds of audio. The blue shape is what's left after limiting; the red shape around it is what was removed. The red line hanging from the top is the gain reduction: the further it dips, the harder Liminal is working. The dashed orange lines are the threshold. With Bass first or Multiband, an orange line shows the low band's own gain reduction.
  5. Legend. What each colour means, with the current threshold.

How to read it

  • Short red dips that return to the top between hits: the limiter is catching peaks and letting go. This is what clean limiting looks like.
  • A red line that never gets back to the top: the limiter is working all the time. The master will sound flatter and may pump. Raise the threshold, or aim for a higher PLR.
  • A lot of red under the hits but little elsewhere: the drums are taking the limiting. If they lose their snap, try Punch Guard or a slower attack.

6. Styles

A style is a way of limiting. Choose one from the Style menu under the display; a one-line description sits under the menu, and only the knobs that style uses are shown. If you're not sure, start with Lookahead and only change style when you can hear a problem.

StyleIn one lineBest forLatency
LookaheadSees peaks coming and turns down smoothly before they arrive.Most mastering. The cleanest, and where Bass first works best.The Lookahead time
Punch GuardA lookahead limiter into a clipper: the very tips of hits go to the clipper.Dense, drum-led music that should stay punchy.Lookahead, plus oversampling
MultibandLows and highs limited separately, then a final stage holds the sum.Mixes where a big kick or bass makes everything else duck.Lookahead
BrickwallInstant limiting with no lookahead.Tracking and live monitoring; hard limiting with oversampling.None from limiting
Soft ClipRounds the peaks off for a warmer, driven sound.Character and saturation, or before another limiter.Only from oversampling

Lookahead

How it works. Liminal delays the audio by the Lookahead time so it can see each peak before it arrives, then turns the level down smoothly ahead of it and lets go afterwards. Because it never has to react suddenly, it's the most transparent style: at a few dB of limiting most people can't hear it working.

Start with: Attack 1 to 5 ms, Release 80 to 150 ms with Adaptive release on, Lookahead 5 ms. For kick- and 808-led music, turn on Bass first.

Watch out for: pushing it much past 4 to 6 dB of gain reduction on the loudest hits. Lookahead stays clean, but the whole mix gets turned down around every hit, and the drums start to lose their snap. That's the moment to try Punch Guard.

Punch Guard

How it works. A limiter turns the whole mix down around each loud hit, for tens of milliseconds. A clipper instead shaves just the tip of the waveform, for a fraction of a millisecond. Punch Guard uses both: the lookahead limiter does most of the work, but the top few dB of each peak (set by Clip) are left to a clipper. At the same loudness, the mix ducks less around the drums, so they keep more punch.

Start with: Clip 1 to 3 dB, and 2x or 4x oversampling in Advanced (clipping without oversampling adds harshness). Optimise tries Clip at 1, 2 and 3 dB for you.

Watch out for: too much Clip. Past about 3 dB the clipping starts to crunch, especially on distorted guitars and bright synths. Listen with Hear what's removed: you want short ticks, not a fizzy copy of the song.

Multiband

How it works. The mix is split at the Crossover frequency into lows and highs. Each band has its own lookahead limiter, so a big kick turns down only the lows and a harsh cymbal only the highs. A final stage keeps the recombined signal under the threshold.

Start with: Crossover around 150 Hz (the Two-band smooth preset). Lower it (100 to 120 Hz) to keep the kick's punch out of the low band; raise it if low mids are causing the trouble.

Watch out for: the balance between the bands moving with the music. Heavy multiband limiting can make the bass feel smaller on loud sections. Bass first in Lookahead often does the same job more gently.

Brickwall

How it works. Instant limiting with no lookahead, so it adds no delay of its own. It reacts the moment a peak arrives.

Use it for: recording and live monitoring, where you can't have latency (the Zero latency (tracking) preset), and for deliberately hard limiting with 2x or 4x oversampling (the Brickwall 4x preset). Bass first works here too with no extra latency, although the very start of each kick is still caught by the main limiter.

Watch out for: distortion on bass and kicks when pushed without oversampling. For mastering, Lookahead is cleaner at the same loudness.

Soft Clip

How it works. Instead of turning the level down, it rounds the peaks off. Gently, that's warmth and a little colour; driven hard, it's saturation. Drive sets how hard the signal is pushed into the clipper, from 0 to 24 dB.

Use it for: character, or as the first stage before a cleaner limiter. Use 2x or 4x oversampling to keep it smooth. Warm soft clip and Saturated push are good starting points.

Watch out for: it's the only style that deliberately changes the tone. If you want Liminal to stay out of the way, use Lookahead.

7. The style controls

Every knob shows its value underneath. Drag up or down to turn it, double-click to reset it, and hover for a reminder of what it does.

  1. Style. The limiting style, with a one-line description under it. See Styles.
  2. Input (−12 to +12 dB, default 0). Turns the mix up or down before the limiter. More input means more limiting and a denser sound. With Auto makeup on, Threshold is the better loudness control, so leave Input at 0 unless your mix arrives very quiet or very hot.
  3. Attack (0.1 to 50 ms, default 1). How quickly a peak is turned down. Faster catches more and sounds cleaner on the peaks themselves, but can soften the punch; slower lets the front of each hit through (which the ceiling then catches), so drums keep more snap. 1 to 10 ms suits most masters.
  4. Release (1 to 1000 ms, default 100). How quickly the limiter lets go after a peak. Too fast can distort bass notes (the gain moves within each wave); too slow sounds like pumping, as the mix stays turned down after every hit. 80 to 150 ms suits most masters; slower for speech and gentle acoustic music.
  5. Lookahead (0 to 20 ms, default 5). How far ahead the limiter sees. Longer gives smoother, more transparent limiting but adds latency, which your DAW compensates for automatically. 5 to 10 ms is typical. Shown in Lookahead, Multiband and Punch Guard.
  6. Adaptive release (on by default). When the music is dense and the limiter is working constantly, the release is stretched up to 3x the Release setting so it doesn't pump; on sparse material it stays at the setting. The Release knob becomes the shortest release. Turn it off when you want a fixed release that does exactly what the knob says.
  7. Bass first. See below.

Knobs that only some styles show

  • Clip (Punch Guard, 0.5 to 6 dB, default 2): how much of each peak goes to the clipper instead of the limiter. 1 to 3 dB is clean with oversampling; more starts to crunch.
  • Crossover (Multiband, 60 Hz to 8 kHz, default 150 Hz): where the lows split from the highs.
  • Drive (Soft Clip, 0 to 24 dB, default 0): how hard the signal is pushed into the clipper.

Bass first

  1. Bass first (Lookahead and Brickwall). In most mixes the biggest peaks come from the kick, the 808 or a bass note. A normal limiter turns down the whole mix for those, so hats, vocals and snare duck with every kick. With Bass first on, when a peak comes mostly from the low end, Liminal turns down only the low band. The main limiter still guarantees the threshold. In Lookahead it adds 6 ms of latency and works best; in Brickwall it adds none, but the very start of each kick is still caught by the main limiter.
  2. Bass range (1 to 12 dB, default 6). The most the low band may be turned down on its own. 6 dB is a safe start; 9 to 12 dB for kick-heavy music. Watch the orange part of the GR meter.
  3. Bass freq (60 to 250 Hz, default 120). Where the low band ends. 100 to 150 Hz catches kick and bass; lower keeps the kick's punch (around 100 Hz and up) out of it.

8. Threshold, ceiling and output

The column on the right is where loudness is set. With Auto makeup on (the default), it works like this: lower the Threshold and the track gets louder, while the Ceiling holds the peaks.

  1. Threshold (−40 to 0 dB, default −6). Where limiting starts: everything above the orange line is held down. Drag it up or down. The meter behind it shows the signal going into the limiter, so you can see how much pokes above the line. Learn can set it for you. Double-click to reset.
  2. GR (gain reduction). How much the limiter is turning the signal down right now, including the true-peak ceiling. A few dB on the loudest hits is normal; constant heavy reduction sounds squashed. The white line and the number under it hold the largest recent reduction. With Bass first or Multiband, a second, orange bar shows the low band on its own.
  3. Ceiling (−6 to 0 dBTP, default −1.0). The highest true peak the output can reach. The meter behind it is the output. −1.0 dBTP is the usual choice for streaming; use −2.0 for very loud masters. See Ceiling modes and codecs.
  4. Auto makeup (on by default). Turns the output up automatically so the loudest peaks sit right at the Ceiling. Turn it off to set the output level yourself.
  5. Output (−24 to +24 dB). Sets the output level by hand when Auto makeup is off. The ceiling still catches any peaks. Useful when you want the limiter as a safety net at a fixed level, for example for broadcast or when matching levels across an album.
Threshold or Input? Both make the master denser. With Auto makeup on, use the Threshold: it changes how much is limited and the makeup keeps the output at the ceiling. Input turns the mix up before everything else, which also changes what Learn and the meters see. Leave it at 0 unless the mix arrives very quiet or very hot.

9. Ceiling modes and codecs

When Spotify, YouTube or Apple Music encode your master, the encoder removes the very top of the spectrum, and that pushes peaks up. A master whose true peak is −1.0 dBTP can peak above 0 dB after encoding and distort on the listener's phone. The ceiling mode, in Advanced, decides what the Ceiling protects against:

ModeWhat stays under the ceilingUse it for
True peakThe file's own peaks, including those between samples.Uncompressed delivery: broadcast, CD, a mastering engineer who'll handle the rest.
Streaming (default)Also the peaks a listener gets after Opus (YouTube) and Ogg Vorbis (Spotify) encoding. Learn measures how much your song overshoots through the real encoders and allows for it. No extra latency.Spotify, YouTube, Tidal and most releases.
All codecsAs Streaming, plus a 1 dB margin for MP3 and AAC, whose encoders overshoot a little more.SoundCloud, Apple Music, DJ downloads, anything that might be an MP3.

That's why, after Learn in Streaming mode, the True peak on the Loudness card can sit well under the ceiling while the Codec peak (under More) sits right at it: Liminal has left room for what the encoders add. More in Learn: peaks.

To hear what the encoder does, use Codec check.

Why density and not loudness? The services turn every song to about the same loudness, so a louder master just gets turned down. What listeners hear is how dense it is, and how its peaks survive encoding. More in Learn: density.

11. Listening honestly

Louder always sounds better for a moment, which makes it hard to judge limiting by ear. Liminal has four ways of listening that take loudness out of the comparison. Only one can be on at a time; pressing it again goes back to the normal output.

  • Level match (above the display): your unprocessed mix, turned to the same loudness as the output. Flip it on and off while the loudest part plays. If the limited version sounds smaller, flatter or duller at the same loudness, you're limiting too hard.
  • Hear what's removed (above the display): only what the limiter takes away. Ideally it's faint ticks on the drum hits. Clicks, crunch, bass notes or a recognisable copy of the song mean listeners are losing something.
  • Codec check (Tools): the output through a real Opus encoder and decoder at 128 kbps, what YouTube streams, in real time. Listen for what the codec does to loud hits and the top end. The Codec peak reading switches to the real decoded peak. About 30 ms late, for listening only.
  • Reference track (Tools): a song you like, turned to your output's loudness and following your DAW's playhead, so you compare sound, not volume. Load one in Optimise.
They never end up in your master. The listening modes aren't saved in presets. When your DAW bounces offline, Liminal always outputs the real limited signal, whatever you were listening to. If your DAW records the bounce in real time, turn them off first.

12. The bottom bar

  1. Advanced. Opens the strip of settings you set once and leave. See Advanced.
  2. Summary. The Advanced settings at a glance: oversampling, ceiling mode, stereo or M/S, and dither. Worth a look before a final bounce.
  3. Width. How much of the energy is in the sides (0% is mono). Most mixes sit around 5 to 30%; the bass is usually kept close to 0%. MasterScope reports the same measure.
  1. Correlation. +1 is mono, 0 is very wide, below 0 parts of the mix cancel on mono speakers (phones, some club systems). Above about +0.3 is safe.
  2. Latency. The delay Liminal reports to your DAW, which compensates for it automatically. It depends on the style, Lookahead, Bass first and oversampling.
  3. Snapshot (camera). Copies every setting and the current readings as text you can paste anywhere: a message, a forum post, a chat. A copy is saved in Documents › MasterScope › Liminal › Snapshots. Nothing is sent anywhere.
  4. Reset meters. Clears the loudness, peak and gain-reduction readings so you can measure from the top of the song again.

13. Advanced

Press Advanced to open the strip. A setting that doesn't apply to the current style is greyed out or says so.

  1. Oversampling (Off, 2x, 4x). Runs Brickwall, Soft Clip and Punch Guard at 2 or 4 times the sample rate, so hard limiting and clipping don't add harshness (aliasing). Costs CPU and a little latency. Use 2x or 4x for Punch Guard and Soft Clip. Lookahead and Multiband don't need it.
  2. Linear phase. Keeps the timing of every frequency identical when oversampling, at the cost of more latency. Off is fine for almost everything; turn it on for the final bounce if you're a perfectionist.
  3. 4x on export. When your DAW bounces offline, the limiter runs at 4x oversampling for the cleanest result, while playback uses the setting above to save CPU. The latency is always the 4x figure, so nothing shifts in time between playback and the bounce. A good habit for the final master.
  4. Ceiling mode (True peak, Streaming, All codecs). What the Ceiling protects against. See Ceiling modes and codecs.
  5. M/S. Limits the centre (mid) and the sides separately. Useful when wide elements, such as wide synths or room mics, trigger the limiter and pull the centre down with them. Usually paired with a lower Stereo link (the Wide mix (M/S) preset).
  6. Sidechain (Brickwall and Lookahead). Lets another track, routed to Liminal's sidechain input in your DAW, decide when to limit. A special-purpose tool; leave it off for normal mastering.
  7. Stereo link (0 to 100%, default 100%). How much left and right are turned down together. 100% keeps the stereo image rock solid, best for most music; lower lets each side limit more on its own, which can sound a little louder but lets the image shift on hard hits. Double-click for 100%.
  8. Dither (Off, 24-bit, 16-bit). Adds a tiny amount of noise to hide the distortion of reducing the word length. Turn it on only for the final bounce, and only if your DAW exports without its own dither: 24-bit for 24-bit files, 16-bit for CD-quality or 16-bit files. Never dither twice. Leave it off while mixing.

14. Learn and targets

Learn suggests the threshold that hits a target for your song. It's at the top of the Tools panel.

  1. Target. What Learn aims for. Use − and + for half-dB steps, or click the value for the full list (below).
  2. Learn. Press it, play the whole song (or at least its loudest part), then press Stop. While you play, it shows how long it has been listening; when you stop, a bar shows it calculating. Press Cancel to stop early.
  3. Report. The report from what Learn heard, at the threshold it set: open it in MasterScope or save it. See Reports.
  4. Status. What Learn did: the threshold it set and the loudness and PLR that gives.
  5. Optimise: compare about 30 setups from one listen. More.
  6. Mix Doctor: what in the mix sets the peaks. More.
  7. Spectrum: frequency balance with overlays. More.
  8. Spectrogram: frequency over time, in 2D or 3D. More.
  9. Measure a song: loudness and density of any track. More.
  10. Reference track: hear a song you like, level-matched. More.
  11. Codec check: hear the output through Opus. More.
  12. Session log: record the readings of a play-through into a report. More.

What Learn actually does

While you play, Learn records what comes into Liminal. When you press Stop, it renders that audio through hidden copies of Liminal, with your exact settings, at different thresholds, and picks the one that lands on your target at your ceiling. It then checks the result through the real Opus and Vorbis encoders, so the Streaming ceiling mode knows how much your song overshoots. Finally it restarts the loudness readings so they reflect the new setting. Because it renders the real plug-in, what it predicts is what you'll hear. Undo puts the old threshold back.

Tips for a good Learn. Play the whole song if you can; at least play its loudest section. Learn again whenever you change something before Liminal (the mix, an EQ on the master) or a setting that changes the sound (style, attack, release). Optimise, Mix Doctor, the release pack and Report all use the audio Learn heard, so run Learn first.

15. Optimise, reference, release pack

  1. Optimise. Tries about 30 setups (different attack and release, Multiband, Punch Guard, Bass first, Brickwall) on the loudest 30 seconds Learn heard. Every one is learned to your target and ceiling mode, so they're all equally loud and dense, and they're ranked by punch: how far the hits stand out.
  2. Results. Your current settings on the top row, then the best setups. Punch is the main score; Avg GR is the average gain reduction; PLR confirms they're equally dense; Codec pk is the peak after encoding (the top ones are checked with the real encoders; an asterisk means estimated).
  3. Apply. Uses that setup: Liminal sets the controls and re-learns the threshold on the whole song. Undo goes back. Always listen after applying: punch is a good guide, not a verdict.
  4. Load reference. Loads a released track you like (WAV, AIFF, FLAC or Ogg). Liminal measures its loudness, peaks, density, punch and tone, shown on the Reference line. Then Reference track in Tools plays it level-matched, and the Spectrum can overlay its tonal balance.
  5. Use its density. Sets Learn to aim for the reference's PLR, so your master is as dense as the reference. Press Learn again to apply it.
  6. Clear. Removes the reference.
  7. Make pack. The release pack: learns and writes four masters from one listen: Streaming −14 (safe on every codec), Club −9, Apple Music −16 and YouTube −14 (Opus-safe). Each is a 24-bit WAV with its MasterScope report. Choose a name and the files go next to it.
Is the reference worth copying? A reference is a guide, not automatically better. A clipped or over-limited release isn't a good density target. Open both in MasterScope to see whether the reference itself has problems before you match it.

16. Mix Doctor

Often one part of the mix makes most of the peaks the limiter has to fight: a snare that's too spiky, a kick that's too big, harsh cymbals. Fixing that in the mix lets Liminal work less, and the master gets louder and cleaner. Mix Doctor finds it, from the audio Learn heard.

  1. Read the mix. Analyses what Learn heard. Run Learn first.
  2. The ranges. Six frequency ranges, from Sub (20 to 60 Hz) to Highs (6 kHz and up). For each: the red bar is its share of the peaks and the blue bar its share of the loudness. A range with a much longer red bar than blue makes peaks out of proportion to how loud it sounds. Limiting saved is how much less limiting you'd need if you took 4 dB off that range's loudest 2% of moments. Peaks are says whether they're short hits or sustained notes. The highlighted row is the biggest win.
  3. Advice. The biggest win and what to try in the mix (for example a little snare clipping, or a drum-bus clipper), then where in the song the peaks cluster and where the energy dips.

Make the change in your mix, then Learn again: you should see the gain reduction drop for the same target.

17. Spectrum and spectrogram

Spectrum

  1. Curve / Bars. A smooth 1/6-octave line, or third-octave bars.
  2. Fast / Medium / Slow. How quickly the curve follows the music. Slow shows the overall balance; Fast shows each hit.
  3. Avg. The balance of everything played since you last double-clicked the spectrum: the best guide to the mix's overall tone.
  4. Hold. The loudest each frequency has been recently (falls slowly).
  5. Input. Overlays the signal going into the limiter, at the same gain, so you can see what limiting changes.
  6. Ref. Overlays the reference track's tonal balance, lined up with your average, so you can see where your mix is brighter or heavier. Load a reference in Optimise.

The spectrum comes from an 8192-point FFT and is scaled so pink noise is flat: a well-balanced mix slopes gently, not steeply. Hover for frequency, note and level; double-click to clear the average and peak hold. More on reading it in Learn: tonal balance.

Spectrogram

  1. Output / Input / Delta. What to show: the output, the input, or Delta, only what limiting removes. Delta is a quick way to see which parts of the spectrum the limiter is eating.
  2. 2D / 3D. 2D scrolls frequency (up) over time (across), with brightness for level. 3D shows the last couple of seconds as a landscape.
  3. Freeze. Holds the picture so you can study it.

18. Measure a song

Measure a song measures what plays into Liminal before Liminal touches it: for example a released track on another channel routed into Liminal, or your own unmastered mix. Press Measure, play the track, press Stop at the end.

  1. Measure / Measure again. Starts and stops a measurement.
  2. Readings. Loudness (LUFS), True peak, Density (PLR), the Loudest moment (short-term), Loudness range, and how much Spotify would turn it up or down. A line under them describes the master in words.
  3. Use its loudness as my target. Sets Learn to aim for this song's integrated loudness.
  4. Use its density as my target. Sets Learn to aim for this song's PLR, so your master is as dense, whatever the ceiling. Usually the better choice for streaming.

To measure songs on Spotify, YouTube or anything else your computer plays, use MasterScope Meter, which can send its measurement straight to MasterScope.

19. Reports and Open in MasterScope

Liminal can turn a play-through into a report for MasterScope, which gives a platform-by-platform verdict, a timeline of the song and suggested changes to your Liminal settings. There are two ways to make one:

  • Report (beside Learn): the report from what Learn heard, at the threshold it set. One listen gives you the setting and the report.
  • Session log (Tools): press it, play the song, press it again. Pressing it restarts the loudness and peak readings, so the report covers just that play-through. It records one row of readings per second.

Either way, a small menu offers Open in MasterScope or Save as a file: .json for MasterScope later, .csv for a spreadsheet.

Open in MasterScope hands the report to your browser on this computer only, and opens it there. If the browser asks to let MasterScope reach apps on this device, choose Allow. Liminal also saves a copy in Documents › MasterScope first, so if your browser blocks the hand-over (Safari can), just drop that file on MasterScope.

The round trip with MasterScope
  1. Learn, then press Report and choose Open in MasterScope.
  2. MasterScope opens with the verdict for each platform, a timeline, and suggested changes to your Liminal settings.
  3. Download the suggested preset, use Import preset at the bottom of Liminal's preset menu, and Learn again.

The theme button in the top bar offers five looks: Dark, Light, and three hardware skins, Console, Rack and Vintage. 22. Recipes

Starting points for common jobs. Each assumes Liminal is last on the master bus and you finish by listening with Level match and Hear what's removed.

A single for streaming

  1. Load Streaming: punchy (PLR 10) (or clean (PLR 11) for a more open sound).
  2. Open Tools, check the target says PLR 10, press Learn and play the whole song, then Stop.
  3. Check the Loudness card: PLR around 10, codec peak at or under −1 dB. Listen with Level match.
  4. Optional: run Optimise and try the top result; Undo if you prefer your own.
  5. Bounce with 4x on export on, then Report → Open in MasterScope to check every platform.

Match a reference you love

  1. In Tools → Optimise, Load reference and check its line: if it's clipped or very dense, it may not be worth copying.
  2. Press Use its density, then Learn on your song.
  3. Compare with Reference track in Tools (level-matched) and the Spectrum's Ref overlay for tone.

Loud club or DJ master

  1. Load Club / DJ (−9, every codec): Punch Guard with 4x oversampling and the All codecs ceiling.
  2. Learn. If the drums crunch, lower Clip to 1.5 dB or raise the target to −10.
  3. Check Mix Doctor: at club loudness, taming one spiky range in the mix often saves 2 to 3 dB of limiting.

Kick and 808 make everything duck

  1. In Lookahead, turn on Bass first; start with Bass range 9 dB and Bass freq 100 Hz (or load Kick-heavy (bass first)).
  2. Learn, then watch the display: the orange low-band line should do most of the work.
  3. If it's still pumping, try Multiband with the crossover around 120 Hz.

Acoustic, jazz or classical

  1. Load Streaming: clean (PLR 11) and set the target to PLR 12 or 14.
  2. Learn. Gain reduction should only flick on the loudest moments.
  3. If it's audible at all, raise the target or turn Adaptive release off and lengthen Release.

Podcast or voice

  1. Load Podcast / voice −16: slow release, All codecs ceiling.
  2. Learn on the full episode, then check LRA on the Loudness card: speech that varies a lot needs levelling before Liminal.

Tracking or live, with no latency

  1. Load Zero latency (tracking): Brickwall, no oversampling, true-peak ceiling. Liminal adds no delay from limiting.

23. Troubleshooting

Liminal doesn't show up in my DAW.
On Windows, check that Liminal.vst3 is in C:\Program Files\Common Files\VST3, that your DAW is 64-bit and has VST3 enabled, then rescan plug-ins. On a Mac, check /Library/Audio/Plug-Ins/VST3 (VST3) or /Library/Audio/Plug-Ins/Components (Audio Unit). Logic and GarageBand only check new Audio Units when they start, so quit and reopen them; in Logic, Settings → Plug-in Manager shows whether Liminal passed its check.
Optimise, Mix Doctor or the release pack say to Learn first.
They all use the audio Learn heard. Open Tools, press Learn, play at least a few seconds (ideally the whole song), and press Stop.
The loudness doesn't match my target.
The readings only cover what has played since the last reset. Press Reset meters and play the whole song, or run Learn again if you changed something before the limiter or the style.
It sounds squashed.
Try a higher PLR target (11 or 12), Punch Guard with 2x oversampling, or Optimise. Then check Mix Doctor: often one part of the mix makes most of the peaks.
The kick makes everything duck.
Turn on Bass first (Lookahead style), or try Multiband.
It sounds harsh or crunchy.
Listen with Hear what's removed. In Punch Guard, Soft Clip or Brickwall, turn on 2x or 4x oversampling and lower Clip or Drive. In Lookahead, a slightly slower attack or a longer release usually helps.
The bass distorts.
The release is too short for the bass notes. Raise Release to 100 ms or more, keep Adaptive release on, or turn on Bass first.
The true peak is under the ceiling, so why is the codec peak higher?
Encoders push peaks up. Use the Streaming or All codecs ceiling mode and Learn again; Liminal will leave room for it. See Ceiling modes and codecs.
Open in MasterScope opens the site but not the report.
Liminal hands the report to the browser on this computer only. If the browser asks to let MasterScope reach apps on this device, choose Allow. If it still doesn't open (some browsers, such as Safari, block it), Liminal has already saved a copy in Documents › MasterScope: drop that file on MasterScope.
High CPU or latency.
In Advanced, lower Oversampling or turn off Linear phase while working, and turn on 4x on export so the bounce is still clean. Brickwall has no limiting latency at all, for tracking.
My bounce sounds different from playback.
Check that no listening mode was on during a real-time bounce, and that nothing after Liminal on the master bus changes the level. With 4x on export, playback and bounce are time-aligned but the bounce is oversampled, so it can be very slightly cleaner.
