Mixing and mastering for streaming

Everything you need to finish a track that holds its own next to commercial releases: how loudness is measured, what streaming services do to your music, how to get a fuller mix, and how to master and deliver it. Each chapter shows what to look for in MasterScope and what to do about it in Liminal.

01

How loud should you master?

It's the question every producer asks when a track is nearly done, and most answers give you a single number. The honest answer is a range plus one rule: on streaming services the loudness number matters much less than you think, and the density of the master and its peaks after encoding matter much more.

The short answer
  • Streaming (Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube, Tidal, Amazon, SoundCloud): anywhere from about −14 to −9 LUFS integrated is fine. Choose the density you like, around a PLR of 9 to 11 for punchy, competitive music, and keep the peaks at or under −1 dBTP after encoding. With peaks at −1 dBTP, a PLR of 9 to 11 lands at about −10 to −12 LUFS.
  • Don't master quieter than about −14 unless you want a quiet, open sound on purpose. Several services never turn quiet tracks up.
  • Club and DJ sets: nothing normalises there, so louder masters play louder. −9 to −7 LUFS is typical, with peaks around −0.3 to −1 dBTP.

What each platform does with your track

Most services measure every track's loudness and turn it up or down so songs play at about the same level. This is loudness normalisation. Only Spotify and SoundCloud publish their numbers; the rest are measured by engineers from playback and can change without notice.

Why "−14 LUFS" isn't a target

−14 is where Spotify, YouTube and Tidal play your track, not a level you have to master to. Take two masters of the same song:

MasterFileOn SpotifyWhat you hear
A−14 LUFS, PLR 13plays as isOpen, with big drum hits
B−10 LUFS, PLR 9turned down 4 dBFuller and denser, at the same loudness as A

Both play at −14, so neither is "louder" on Spotify. They sound different because of their density: B's peaks sit closer to its average level, so it feels fuller; A keeps more punch and space. Neither is wrong. That's why the better question is how dense should my master be, which you can choose by ear and with a reference track. The Density and PLR chapter goes into how to choose.

Mastering much louder than about −9 LUFS for streaming usually costs you: the service turns it down to −14 anyway, and what's left is the side effect of the heavy limiting, which is flatter drums and less space.

Why does my song sound quieter than others on Spotify?

If a track sounds smaller or quieter than the songs around it on a playlist, it's almost always one of these:

  1. It's quieter than −14 and its peaks are already near the top. Spotify only turns a quiet track up until its peaks reach −1 dBTP. A master at −18 LUFS with peaks at −1 dBTP can't be turned up at all, so it plays 4 dB quieter than everything else. On YouTube, Tidal and Amazon, quiet tracks are never turned up. The fix is more density: limit a little harder so the loudness comes up while the peaks stay put.
  2. It's at −14 but much less dense than the genre. At the same loudness, a very open master (PLR 13 or more) can sound smaller next to dense, competitive releases. If that's not the sound you want, aim for a PLR closer to the references you love.
  3. Too much low end. A huge sub uses up a lot of the peak level but adds little to the measured loudness, so the limiter has to turn the whole mix down to make room for it. See The low end.
  4. It was mastered so loud it got squashed. At −6 LUFS it's turned down 8 dB, and what's left is a flat, distorted version of the song. Dial the limiting back.

A good starting point by style

MusicTypical PLRTypical loudness
EDM, club, heavy rock and metal5 to 8 dB−9 to −6 LUFS
Pop, hip-hop, dance, rock8 to 11 dB−12 to −9 LUFS
Singer-songwriter, R&B, indie10 to 12 dB−13 to −11 LUFS
Acoustic, jazz, classical12 dB or more−18 to −13 LUFS

Ranges are typical of released music, not rules. Genres overlap, and the best guide is a reference track you like, compared at matched loudness.

Loudness and PLR are linked through the peak level: loudness ≈ true peak − PLR. With peaks at −1 dBTP, a PLR of 10 gives about −11 LUFS. That's why picking the density and the ceiling settles the loudness for you.

Don't forget the peaks

Whatever loudness you choose, keep the true peak at or under −1 dBTP. Streaming services convert your file to Opus, Ogg Vorbis, AAC or MP3, and those encoders push peaks up, often by 1 to 2 dB on loud, bright masters. If they go over full scale, the listener hears distortion that isn't in your file. Louder masters need more room, which is why SoundCloud asks for −2 dBTP above −14 LUFS. The Peaks chapter explains true peak and the codec peak, the peak after encoding, which is the one that really matters.

02

Why does my master sound worse on Spotify?

Your master sounds great in your DAW, then you hear it on Spotify and it's quieter, duller, harsher or smaller. Nothing is wrong with Spotify, and usually nothing is badly wrong with your master either. Between your bounce and the listener's ears, three things change: the level it plays at, the encoding it goes through, and the gear and settings it's played on. Each has a fix.

The short answer
  • It's turned down. A master louder than −14 LUFS is played quieter, so next to your loud DAW bounce it sounds weaker. Compare at matched loudness before you judge.
  • It's encoded. Most listeners hear a lossy version (Ogg Vorbis or AAC), which can smear hi-hats, narrow wide sounds and push peaks into distortion. Keep peaks under −1 dBTP after encoding.
  • It's on different gear and settings. Phone speakers, Bluetooth, the app's equaliser and its volume level setting all change what people hear.

1. It plays at a different level

Spotify turns every track to about −14 LUFS. If your master is at −8, it's turned down 6 dB, and when you flip from your DAW at full level to Spotify, the drop sounds like a loss of energy. Louder nearly always sounds "better" for a few seconds, which is why this comparison fools everyone. See How loud should you master?

Three details make it more confusing:

  • Quiet, dynamic masters can be limited by Spotify. In the app's Loud setting (−11 LUFS), Spotify raises quiet tracks regardless of their peaks and runs a limiter to stop them clipping. If you master quietly on purpose, some listeners hear Spotify's limiting rather than yours.
  • Quiet masters may not be turned up at all. At the normal setting, Spotify only raises a track until its peaks reach −1 dBTP. A master at −18 LUFS with peaks at −1 dBTP plays 4 dB quieter than its neighbours.
  • Some players don't normalise. Spotify's web player and third-party devices such as TVs and smart speakers play files without normalisation, so the same song can sound louder in one place than another.
Compare fairly. Turn your bounce down to −14 LUFS before comparing it with a released track on Spotify, or drop both into MasterScope's Compare tab, which plays them at the same loudness. In Liminal, Match plays your unlimited mix at the output's loudness so you hear only what the limiter changed.

2. It's encoded

Most listeners don't hear your WAV. Spotify streams a compressed version whose quality depends on the listener's settings:

Spotify settingQuality
Low / Normal / Highabout 24 / 96 / 160 kbit/s
Very high (Premium)about 320 kbit/s
Web playerAAC 128 (Free) or 256 kbit/s (Premium)
Lossless (Premium)FLAC up to 24-bit, 44.1 kHz

From Spotify's audio quality page, checked October 2026. Lossless started rolling out to Premium in September 2025 and has to be switched on by each listener on each device, so most plays are still lossy. "Automatic" lowers the quality on a weak connection.

Lossy encoders throw away detail the ear is least likely to miss, and some masters suffer more than others:

  • Peaks go up. Encoders cut the very top of the spectrum and that reshapes the waveform, so peaks often rise 1 to 2 dB on loud, bright masters. Past full scale, that's distortion that isn't in your file. This is the most common cause of a "crunchy" Spotify version. See Peaks: sample, true and codec.
  • Bright, busy top end smears. Dense hi-hats, cymbals, noise risers and bright reverbs are the hardest things to encode, especially at lower bitrates. They can turn swirly or grainy.
  • Very wide stereo narrows or turns phasey. Encoders save bits on side information, so extreme widening and out-of-phase tricks in the highs can collapse or warble. See Stereo and mono.
  • Heavy clipping gets worse. Encoders struggle with the extra harmonics of hard clipping, so an already-clipped master degrades more than a cleanly limited one.
Hear it before your listeners do. Liminal's Opus button plays your master through a real streaming encoder in real time, and its Streaming ceiling keeps the peaks after Opus and Vorbis under your ceiling. MasterScope shows the codec peak for any file you drop on it.

3. It's played on different gear

  • Phones and laptop speakers have almost no low end. A mix whose energy is all in the sub sounds thin and quiet on them. Check that the bass still reads in the 100 to 300 Hz range. See The low end.
  • Bluetooth re-encodes audio again on the way to the headphones, adding a second layer of lossy compression.
  • The listener's settings. Spotify's app has an equaliser and a volume level setting (Loud, Normal, Quiet). If your Spotify sounds odd, check yours first: an equaliser left on can change everything you hear.

4. The upload chain

Upload a 24-bit (or 16-bit) WAV at the sample rate you mixed at, and don't upload an MP3 or AAC: encoding twice makes artefacts much worse. Spotify's lossless tier goes up to 44.1 kHz, so higher-rate files are converted, which is harmless. Don't add a limiter or clipper after your final ceiling, and only dither once. See Exporting and delivery.

Checklist when it sounds worse on Spotify
  • Compared at the same loudness? Turn your bounce to −14 LUFS first.
  • Codec peak at or under −1 dBTP? If not, lower the ceiling or use a codec-safe ceiling.
  • Top end very bright and busy, or stereo very wide in the highs? Ease it a little.
  • Very loud or heavily clipped? Back the limiting off; it's turned down anyway.
  • Very quiet and dynamic? Expect some listeners to hear Spotify's own limiter.
  • Uploaded a lossless WAV? Never an MP3.
  • Listening settings normal? Equaliser off, volume level on Normal, quality on Very high or Lossless.

03

How loud are the songs you're up against?

Before you decide how loud and how dense to make your master, it helps to know where the music you're competing with sits. Not the loudness of the whole charts, but the three or four songs your listeners will hear next to yours. You can measure them yourself in a few minutes, straight from Spotify or YouTube, and use the numbers as your target.

The short answer
  • Measure a few songs in your genre with MasterScope Meter (free) while they play, or load a file of a released track on MasterScope's Compare tab.
  • Look at density (PLR), not just loudness. On Spotify everything plays at about −14 LUFS, so what you'll hear is how full and dense each song is at that level.
  • Use the result as your target. Send it to MasterScope to compare with your mix, or aim Liminal's Learn at its loudness or density.

What to measure, and what each number tells you

  • Integrated loudness (LUFS): the average loudness of the whole song. It tells you how hard the master was pushed, and how much Spotify, YouTube and the others turn it down.
  • True peak (dBTP): the highest point of the waveform. Measured from a stream it's often a little above the master's own peak, because lossy encoding adds a small overshoot.
  • PLR (peak to loudness ratio): true peak minus integrated loudness. The best single number for density: low PLR means dense and loud, high PLR means punchy and open. See Density and PLR.
  • Loudest moment: the highest short-term (3 second) loudness, usually the biggest chorus or drop. It shows how hard the loudest part is pushed.
  • Loudness range (LU): how much the loudness moves between quiet and loud sections. A small number means the song stays at one level; a bigger number means it builds and drops.

How to measure songs from Spotify or YouTube

  1. Turn off normalisation. In the Spotify app's settings, switch off Normalise volume, otherwise you measure Spotify's turn-down instead of the master. The Spotify web player never normalises, so it's ready as it is. Loud songs: if MasterScope says a measurement hit 0 dBFS, the player cut its peaks off at full volume. Measure the song a second time with normalisation on: Spotify turns it down, so the true peak and density (PLR) read correctly. Take the loudness from the first measurement.
  2. Set the player's own volume to 100% (the slider in Spotify, or the YouTube player). Your computer's main volume doesn't matter.
  3. Measure the app, not the speakers. Many laptops process their sound on the way to the speakers: Waves MaxxAudio, Dolby, DTS, Realtek speaker tuning or "loudness equalisation" can cut the bass, compress the music and change every reading. MasterScope Meter listens to Spotify (or any app you choose) directly, before that processing. In the browser, play the song in a tab and share that tab.
  4. Measure the whole song. In MasterScope Meter, press Measure at the start and Stop at the end. A single chorus reads louder than the song's integrated loudness.
Only numbers, never the audio. MasterScope Meter keeps the measurements and nothing else: no recording, no copies of the music. It's a meter, the same as watching the loudness readout on a mixing desk.

Using the numbers

Compare like with like: measure three or four songs that sound the way you want yours to sound, and look at the range. If they sit between PLR 8 and 10, a master at PLR 9 will sit comfortably among them on Spotify, whatever their exact LUFS. If yours comes out much more dynamic (say PLR 14), it will sound smaller next to them at the same loudness; if it's much denser (PLR 6), it may sound flat and tiring.

For the full picture, press Export in the Meter and drop the file on MasterScope: you get the song's own report, the same as for a file, then Compare my track with it sets its loudness, density, stereo width and tone against your mix, with its tonal curve on the Tone tab. (On the Compare tab, Load reference takes an export too.) For just the headline numbers, press Save, then Compare. In Liminal, Tools → Measure a song does the same inside your DAW, with a button to use the result as Learn's target.

04

How streaming plays your music

Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube, Tidal and the other big services measure the loudness of every track and turn it up or down so that everything plays at about the same level. This is called loudness normalisation. Spotify, for example, adjusts tracks to −14 LUFS by default.

That changes the old rules. A master pushed to −8 LUFS doesn't play louder on Spotify than one at −14: it is turned down 6 dB, and all that is left of the extra loudness is the limiting that was used to get there. What decides how big a track sounds next to others is no longer how loud the file is, but how full and clear it is at the same loudness.

What this means for you
  • On streaming, extra loudness is taken away again. Density (how full the track sounds) is what you keep.
  • A track that can't reach −14 without its peaks going over −1 dBTP plays quieter than everything around it on Spotify.
  • Where nothing normalises (Bandcamp, downloads, DJ sets), a louder file does play louder. You can master for both: see Density and PLR.
05

Measuring loudness: LUFS

LUFS (loudness units relative to full scale) measures loudness the way we hear it, following the ITU-R BS.1770 standard. Before measuring, the signal is filtered to match the ear: very low bass counts for less, and the upper mids and highs count for a little more. That is why a mix heavy in sub bass can read surprisingly quiet, and why a little more presence reads louder.

Integrated loudness
The loudness of the whole track: the number streaming services normalise to. Silence and very quiet passages are left out (anything below −70 LUFS, then anything more than 10 LU below the average), so adding silence or a quiet intro doesn't change it.
Short-term loudness
Loudness over a sliding 3-second window. It shows how loud each section is. The loudest short-term reading is how hard your chorus or drop hits.
Momentary loudness
The same over 0.4 seconds. It moves with individual hits and words.
Loudness range (LRA)
How much the short-term loudness varies across the song, in LU, ignoring the quietest 10% and loudest 5%. Roughly, under 5 LU is very consistent and 6 to 10 is typical of songs with breakdowns.
Energy dips are worth a look. A breakdown is meant to drop; a verse that sags 5 LU below the rest will feel small next to other releases at −14. More energy in the arrangement or a little level automation fixes it.
06

Peaks: sample, true and codec

Loudness is about the body of the sound; peaks are its highest points. Three readings matter, each stricter than the last.

Sample peak (dBFS)
The highest sample value in the file. 0 dBFS is the top of the scale.
True peak (dBTP)
The highest point of the reconstructed waveform, including peaks that fall between samples. These "inter-sample peaks" can be well over the sample peak on dense, bright masters. Measured by oversampling.
Codec peak
The true peak after lossy encoding (Opus on YouTube, Ogg Vorbis on Spotify, AAC on Apple Music, MP3 on SoundCloud and downloads). Encoders remove the very top of the spectrum and add their own noise, and that reshapes the waveform so its peaks rise, often by 1 to 2 dB on loud, bright or clipped masters. MasterScope estimates it by filtering at 16 kHz and measuring the true peak; Liminal goes further and runs your song through the real Opus and Vorbis encoders.

The usual ceiling is −1 dBTP. Spotify recommends keeping true peak below −2 dBTP when your master is louder than −14 LUFS, because louder masters overshoot more when encoded. The codec estimate tells you directly: if it reads above −1 dBTP, the encoded version may distort wherever it plays at full level.

Liminal's ceiling modes

Under its Ceiling fader Liminal has three ceiling modes. True peak is the classic ceiling. Streaming also keeps the peaks a listener gets after Opus and Vorbis encoding under the ceiling: when you press Learn, Liminal encodes and decodes your song with the real encoders, measures how far its peaks rise, and calibrates its limiter to that song. All codecs adds a 1 dB margin for MP3 and AAC, which overshoot a little more (across 26 test masters, 128 kbps MP3 peaked at most 1.5 dB above Opus and Vorbis, and under 1 dB nine times out of ten).

To hear it, open Tools in Liminal and press Codec check: the output plays through a real Opus encoder at 128 kbps, and the codec peak readout shows the decoded peak.

A real example. A master at −12.7 LUFS with true peak at −1.1 dBTP looked safe, but its codec peak was +1.3 dBTP: the clipper used on it left sharp, bright edges that the encoder reshaped. Lowering the ceiling by 2.4 dB brought the encoded peak to −1.1. Always check the codec reading on louder masters.
07

Density and PLR

The peak-to-loudness ratio (PLR) is true peak minus integrated loudness. It measures how far the peaks stand above the body of the track, so it is the clearest single number for density.

PLRSoundTypical of
under 8 dBSquashed: drums lose their snap, the track can sound smallerVery loud club and EDM masters
8 to 12 dBFull and even, with punch intactMost pop, hip-hop, dance and rock releases
12 to 13 dBOpen and punchy, less denseAcoustic, jazz, older-style masters
over 13 dBCan't reach −14 LUFS with peaks at −1 dBTPUnmastered mixes

Because PLR is peak minus loudness, choosing a PLR and a ceiling fixes the loudness: target loudness = ceiling − PLR. A PLR of 10 with a ceiling of −2 dBTP lands at −12 LUFS. A PLR of 13 with a ceiling of −1 lands at −14.

Learn by density. In Liminal, open Tools, click Learn's target and choose a density instead of a loudness (PLR 8 to 14). Learn then finds the threshold that gives your song that PLR at your ceiling, whatever loudness that turns out to be. Load a reference in Optimise (Tools) and press Use its density to match a track you like.

Does denser sound louder?

A little, and only up to a point. LUFS already measures loudness the way we hear it, so at −14 two tracks are about equally loud. A denser master sounds fuller and more constant, which reads as "bigger" at low volume. Below a PLR of about 8, the drum hits lose impact and the track starts to sound smaller, even though the meter says it is just as loud. Judge density by ear at matched loudness, and compare against a reference in your genre.

Louder masters by choice

You don't have to master to exactly −14. A master at −12 with a PLR of 10 is turned down 2 dB on Spotify and sounds the same there as a −14 master with the same density, but it plays louder in DJ sets, on Bandcamp and as a download. So pick your PLR by ear, set the ceiling (−1 dBTP, or lower for louder masters and anything clipped), and let the loudness follow. Don't turn the master down after the limiter just to read −14: streaming services will do that for you, and the file will be quieter everywhere else.

08

Tonal balance

Tonal balance is how the energy is spread from sub to air. MasterScope compares each octave with pink noise, which has equal energy per octave and sounds balanced to the ear, so a flat line means balanced and the bars show where your mix leans.

Tilt
The overall slope from 63 Hz to 8 kHz. Steeper than about −3 dB per octave sounds dark and woolly; above 0 sounds bright and thin.
Low-mid build-up (250 to 500 Hz)
Where mud and boxiness live. Many parts overlap here; small cuts on the busiest ones clear space.
Presence (2 to 5 kHz)
Where the ear is most sensitive. A mix with too little presence sounds smaller at the same loudness. Most releases sit around 3 to 6 dB below the low mids here.
Sub (20 to 60 Hz)
Felt more than heard. Phones and small speakers can't play it, but it still takes up peak room.
Use a reference. Genre matters more than any rule of thumb. Load a released track you like on MasterScope's Compare tab: both play at −14 so loudness can't fool you, and MasterScope lists where your track differs most and whether the gap is tone, density or arrangement.
09

The low end

The kick and bass carry most of a track's energy, so they decide a lot about how clean and loud it can be.

  • Give the kick its own moment. If the bass or 808 is still sounding when the kick hits, the two add up into bigger peaks and the low end blurs. Shorter bass notes or sidechain ducking from the kick fixes it.
  • Keep the low end in the centre. Below about 150 Hz, stereo content loses weight on mono systems (phones, many club rigs) and sounds loose. Make it mono, or narrow the parts that sit there.
  • Measure before you cut. Sub bass counts for little in LUFS but can take up peak room, so cutting it can help. It doesn't always: if your loudest peaks come from somewhere else, cutting the low end only makes the track thinner. MasterScope's Louder at −14 panel shows which is true for your track.
10

Stereo and mono

A wide mix sounds big on headphones, but much of the world listens in mono or near-mono: phone speakers, Bluetooth speakers, clubs. Anything that cancels in mono disappears there.

Phase correlation
+1 means the channels are identical (mono), 0 means unrelated, below 0 means parts cancel when summed. Most mixes sit between about +0.5 and +0.9.
Stereo width
The share of energy in the side (left minus right) signal. Bass should be close to 0%; pads, reverbs and doubles can be wide.
11

Dynamics in the mix

A master can only be as dense as the mix allows cleanly. If a few parts poke out far above the rest, the master limiter has to pull the whole track down every time they hit, and that is what you hear as pumping and lost punch. Controlling those parts in the mix lets the limiter do less for the same loudness.

The tools

Compressor
Turns a part down when it goes over a threshold, by a ratio. Attack sets how quickly it reacts (slower lets the transient through), release how quickly it lets go. Use it to even out vocals, bass and buses.
Clipper
Shaves off the very tops of waveforms instantly. On a drum bus, a dB or two of clipping takes the sharpest transients off with little audible change. It adds bright harmonics, so watch the codec peak on the master.
De-esser
A compressor that only reacts to harsh "s" and "t" sounds, or any bright, spiky part: hats, a bright synth.
Saturation
Gentle distortion that adds harmonics and density to sustained parts without the pumping of heavy compression.

Find what the limiter is fighting

MasterScope's Louder at −14 panel splits your track into six ranges and shows two things for each: its share of the height of the loudest peaks, and its share of the loudness. A range with a long amber bar and a short blue one spends peak room without adding much loudness. It then tests the fix on your actual audio: taking 4 dB off the loudest moments in one range at a time and measuring how much less limiting the track then needs.

It also tells you whether those peaks are drum hits or sustained parts, and suggests the matching tool: a drum-bus clipper for hits, a de-esser or compression for a bright vocal or synth. For the clearest answer, analyse your mix with the master limiter switched off.

Does the level going into the limiter matter? Not for the sound. In a 32-bit floating-point DAW, turning up the input and lowering the threshold by the same amount gives an identical result. What matters is where the loudness comes from: tame peaks in the mix and the limiter has less to do. The exception is analogue-style plugins (tape, consoles, saturators), which do react to how hard they are driven.
12

How a limiter works

A mastering limiter does two jobs: it stops peaks going over a ceiling, and it lets you raise the overall level until the track is as dense as you want. Every limiter has the same core controls.

Threshold and makeup
Lowering the threshold makes the limiter work harder and, with makeup gain, raises the level. Liminal's Learn sets the threshold for you to land on a loudness target.
Ceiling
The highest level the output can reach, in dBTP. −1 is the usual choice; −2 or lower for louder masters, and lower still if the codec peak reads high.
Attack and lookahead
Lookahead lets the limiter see a peak coming and turn down smoothly before it arrives, instead of clipping it. Attack is how gradually it does so. Very short attack (under 1 ms) grabs the front of every drum hit and flattens the punch; around 5 to 10 ms keeps it.
Release
How quickly the gain recovers after a peak. Short release is denser but can distort or pump; long release is smoother but can duck the track after each hit. Adaptive release stretches it automatically on dense passages.
Stereo link
How much the two channels share gain reduction. 100% keeps the stereo image steady; lower lets each side be limited on its own.
Oversampling
Runs the limiter at a higher internal rate so it catches inter-sample peaks and reduces distortion, at the cost of CPU.

Liminal's modes

Lookahead
The default for mastering: smooth, transparent gain reduction with adjustable attack, release and lookahead.
Brickwall
Zero added latency and an absolute ceiling. Useful while tracking or for a final safety stage.
Soft Clip
Rounds the peaks off instead of turning the track down. It keeps the gain reduction low and the dynamics lively, but the clipped edges raise the codec peak, so it suits masters with headroom under the ceiling.
Multiband
Limits the lows and highs separately, so a big kick doesn't pull the vocal down with it.
Punch Guard
Liminal's Punch Guard mode: the lookahead limiter does the work but leaves the very tips of the peaks to an oversampled clipper. A clipper shaves a millisecond off a drum hit where a limiter turns the whole mix down around it for 50 ms or more, so at the same loudness the drums keep more snap. On test mixes, 2 to 3 dB of clip gave 1 to 1.5 dB more punch than Lookahead alone.
Bass first
Takes up to a set amount off the low band before the main limiter, so the kick and bass don't trigger limiting across the whole mix. Use only as much as you need: on most mixes 2 to 3 dB is plenty.

Punch, and finding the best setup

Liminal's Loudness panel shows Punch: how far the hits stand out from the music around them (the loudest 10 ms of each second against its average), with how much the limiter takes off compared with your mix. Losing under 2 dB is clean; over 4 dB and the drums lose their snap.

Rather than guess attack and release, press Optimise. It tries about 30 setups on the loudest 30 seconds Learn heard, learns every one to the same target and ceiling so they are equally loud, and ranks them by punch. Apply the one you like and Liminal re-learns it on the whole song.

Listen at matched loudness. Liminal's Match button plays your unlimited mix at the same loudness as the output, so you hear only what the limiter changes, not just "louder". Delta plays only what is being removed: if you can hear drums or vocals in it, the limiter is doing too much.
13

A mastering workflow

This is the loop that works with MasterScope and Liminal. It starts in the mix, because that is where the biggest gains are.

  1. Analyse the mix. Bounce it with no limiter on the master and open it in MasterScope. Fix anything in "What to work on" first: mud, harshness, wide bass, energy dips, and whatever the Louder at −14 panel says the limiter would be fighting.
  2. Put Liminal last on the master. Start from the factory preset Streaming: punchy (PLR 10), or Lookahead mode with attack around 10 ms and release around 60 ms. Add Bass first only if the low end is triggering the limiter.
  3. Set the ceiling. −1 dB with the ceiling mode on Streaming (or All codecs if the track goes to SoundCloud, DJ pools or Apple Music). Liminal keeps the decoded peaks under it, so you no longer need to guess how much lower to go for loud masters.
  4. Choose your density. Open Tools, click Learn's target and pick a PLR. For a full, competitive sound, aim for 9 to 11.
  5. Press Learn and play the song once. Liminal listens, then renders what it heard at different thresholds and sets the one that lands on your target.
  6. Save the report. Press Report and save the .json, then drop it on MasterScope to see the result, the gain reduction over time and your settings. No second play-through needed. (The Session log in Tools does the same for a live play-through after you change settings by hand.)
  7. Check the punch, then Optimise. If the Punch reading (Loudness card, More) shows the limiter taking more than 3 or 4 dB off the hits, open Optimise from Tools and try the top setup. Mix Doctor shows which part of the mix makes the peaks, so you know what to fix at the source.
  8. Listen with Level match, Hear what's removed and Codec check. Level match hears your mix at the same loudness, Hear what's removed only what the limiter takes, Codec check what a streaming listener gets. The Spectrogram's Delta view shows which frequencies are being squashed.
  9. Export and check the final file. Open the bounced master in MasterScope: true peak, codec peak, density and tone. Then load a reference on the Compare tab.
14

Exporting and delivery

  • Format. WAV or AIFF at the sample rate you mixed at. 24-bit is best; 16-bit is fine for most distributors.
  • Dither once, at the end. When reducing to 16-bit, add dither (Liminal's Dither switch) on the final export only. Never dither twice.
  • No changes after the limiter. Don't normalise on export, and don't turn the master down afterwards unless you mean to. Every change after the limiter changes the peaks it worked to control.
  • Check the encoded peaks. Keep the codec peak at or below −1 dB. With Liminal's Streaming or All codecs ceiling mode, Learn takes care of it; otherwise lower the ceiling and Learn again at the same PLR, or tame the brightest peaks in the mix.
  • One master or two? One master at your chosen density works everywhere. If you want more, Liminal's Release pack (in Optimise) learns and writes four masters from one listen: Streaming −14 (safe on every codec), Club −9, Apple Music −16 and YouTube −14, each a 24-bit WAV with its report.
  • Leave a little silence at the start and end so nothing is cut off, and let reverb tails finish naturally.
15

Release checklist

  • Integrated loudness where you chose it (−14, or louder by choice)
  • True peak at or below −1 dBTP (−2 for louder masters)
  • Codec peak estimate at or below −1 dBTP
  • PLR in the range you want, chosen by ear at matched loudness
  • No clipped samples, no DC offset
  • Tonal balance close to a good reference in your genre
  • Bass centred; phase correlation positive; nothing disappears in mono
  • Drums still punch after limiting (listen with Match and Delta)
  • Sections sit where you intend: no unintended energy dips
  • Correct format, sample rate and bit depth, dithered once
  • Listened on headphones, a phone speaker and a car or club system

MasterScope's PDF report collects all of these in one place, so it is also a good document to keep with each release.

16

Glossary

dBFS
Decibels relative to full scale, the top of the digital range (0 dBFS).
dBTP
Decibels true peak: the waveform's real peak including inter-sample peaks.
LUFS / LU
Loudness units relative to full scale, and the difference between two loudness readings (1 LU = 1 dB).
PLR
Peak-to-loudness ratio: true peak minus integrated loudness.
Crest factor
Peak minus RMS level; a rougher cousin of PLR.
Gain reduction (GR)
How much a compressor or limiter is turning the signal down, in dB.
Headroom
The space between the highest peak and the ceiling or 0 dBFS.
Inter-sample peak
A peak between two samples, higher than either, that appears when the signal is converted back to analogue or resampled.
K-weighting
The filter LUFS uses to match the ear before measuring loudness.
Lookahead
A short delay that lets a limiter react before a peak arrives.
Mid/side
Splitting stereo into what is shared (mid) and what differs (side) between the channels.
Normalisation
A service turning tracks up or down to a common loudness.
Pink noise
Noise with equal energy per octave; sounds balanced, and is the reference for tonal balance.
Sidechain
Using one signal (often the kick) to control a processor on another (often the bass).
